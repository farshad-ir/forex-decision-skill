# Structural Understanding Under Uncertainty

## Can We Find Structure Within Uncertainty?

It seems that we can now describe more clearly what we are trying
to achieve in this project.

We are dealing with situations in which structure exists, but
uncertainty does not disappear. These two conditions are not
necessarily contradictory. We may be able to recognize relationships
between the elements of a situation, follow its development, and
build a better understanding from the available evidence. Yet we
may still be unable to know the final outcome with certainty.

This is not a problem unique to financial markets. In medicine,
a physician works with symptoms, test results, and medical history.
The physician attempts to develop a coherent understanding of the
patient's condition. Even with an accurate assessment, however,
the outcome of treatment is not guaranteed.

Military operations present a similar challenge. A commander must
make decisions with incomplete information, under changing
conditions, and without knowing in advance how an opponent will
respond. The commander must build a structured understanding of
the available information and act accordingly. A better understanding
of the situation, however, does not guarantee the outcome of an
operation.

We encounter similar conditions in many everyday decisions.
We rarely possess all the information we need, and we cannot
foresee every consequence of our choices. Nevertheless, we do
not simply stop making decisions. We try to understand the
situation better, consider different possibilities, and make
choices while acknowledging the limitations of our knowledge.

We should therefore distinguish between two things:

**Understanding the structure of a situation and knowing its
ultimate outcome are not the same.**

Recognizing structure in data and events does not mean that the
future has become visible to us. Structural understanding can
improve our judgment, but it cannot, by itself, eliminate uncertainty.

## The Market: Structured, but Not Predictable with Certainty

In our project, we examine this problem through the EUR/USD
price chart on the one-hour timeframe.

At first glance, a chart is simply a sequence of candlesticks
forming one after another. Looking more closely, however, we can
identify relationships among them: turning points, price paths,
areas of movement and consolidation, small and large boxes,
breakouts, returns, and reactions to structures formed earlier.

These relationships allow us to see the chart as more than a
sequence of independent candlesticks. We can examine price movement
as a structure of related events and understand the current position
of price in relation to its previous path and surrounding structures.

But this understanding does not mean that we know what price
will do next.

The current structure may be consistent with a continuation of
an upward movement, yet price may move downward. A breakout from
a range may initially appear to signal an escape, only for price
to return inside that range. A single box may have little
significance on its own, but when considered alongside neighboring
boxes and the path of price, it may become part of a more
important structure.

Our interpretation of an event at the time it occurs may also
differ from our interpretation after subsequent candlesticks have
formed. This difference does not necessarily invalidate the
original observation. New evidence may have emerged, allowing us
to interpret the earlier event more accurately.

For this reason, a structure cannot simply be equated with a
certain outcome. Structure is part of the evidence available to
us. Our interpretation of that structure is our understanding
of what the evidence may mean. The actual market outcome may
still differ from our expectations.

**The purpose of structural understanding is not to make the
future certain, but to make the present more precise, coherent,
and understandable.**

## From Observation to Interpretation, and from Interpretation
## to Action

To preserve this distinction in our approach, we should separate
three levels of reasoning.

### 1. Structural Understanding: What Can We Observe?

At this stage, we attempt to identify and describe existing
relationships without immediately judging what they imply about
the future.

On a price chart, this may involve identifying turning points,
tracing price movement, locating structural areas, examining
overlapping boxes, and recording how price crosses or returns
through their boundaries.

The central question at this level is not:

"Will price move upward?"

It is:

"What structure currently exists, and how is price positioned
in relation to it?"

Of course, observation itself can be mistaken. An algorithm
may identify a turning point incorrectly or separate two
structures that should have been considered together. Structural
understanding must therefore remain open to examination and testing.

### 2. Interpretation: What Might This Structure Mean?

Once we have identified a structure, we can begin to consider
its possible meaning.

For example, a move beyond the boundary of a range may indicate
the beginning of a new movement. But if price immediately
returns to the range, the initial interpretation may need to
be reconsidered. In such circumstances, evidence supporting
the breakout hypothesis matters, but so does evidence that
weakens it.

A single structure may be consistent with several interpretations.
These interpretations may not all be equally supported by the
available evidence.

A system should therefore avoid selecting one narrative while
ignoring contradictory evidence. It should make clear what was
observed, how the observation has been interpreted, and what
evidence might strengthen or weaken that interpretation.

Interpretation is not an established truth. It is an attempt
to understand what the available evidence may mean.

### 3. Decision and Action: What Should We Do With This Understanding?

Even if we have identified a structure correctly and developed
a reasonable interpretation, we still cannot be certain about
the outcome of our actions.

In trading, one scenario may be better supported by the evidence
than another. Nevertheless, entering a trade still depends on
the entry price, stop-loss placement, potential reward, the
risk-to-reward ratio, and the broader market conditions.

Sometimes the appropriate action is to buy. Sometimes it is
to sell. Sometimes it is to wait and refrain from entering a trade.

A trading decision should not be treated as the automatic
output of structural recognition alone. There is a meaningful
distance between understanding a situation, interpreting it,
and accepting the risk of a trade.

This distinction matters because even the best understanding
and the most reasonable interpretation cannot guarantee the
outcome of an action.

## Uncertainty Cannot Be Eliminated

There is a point that must be stated explicitly:

**Structural understanding does not reduce uncertainty to zero.
Even with a better understanding of the situation, a trader
still accepts risk and remains exposed to the possibility of
error and loss. In this sense, trading remains a form of gambling
on an unknown outcome, although that gamble can be undertaken
on the basis of structural understanding, examination of evidence,
and risk management.**

This statement does not deny the value of analysis, nor does
it suggest that all decisions are equally sound. There is an
important difference between acting without understanding or
examination and acting after studying the evidence, evaluating
alternative scenarios, and defining the limits of acceptable risk.

But this difference does not eliminate uncertainty. Even a
decision based on appropriate evidence may lead to an unfavorable
outcome.

Conversely, a decision based on weak analysis may produce a
favorable result. The outcome of a single event, by itself,
does not establish whether the decision-making process was sound.

We must therefore distinguish between the quality of our
understanding and interpretation, on the one hand, and the
ultimate outcome of our actions, on the other.

We are not looking for a method that predicts the outcome
correctly every time. We have no such guarantee. We want to
develop a process that organizes evidence more effectively,
explains its interpretations, acknowledges the limitations
of its knowledge, and allows decisions to be examined and revised.

## What Is the Goal of This Project?

The goal of this project is not to build a machine that knows
the future.

We want to develop a system that can read the market in a
structured way, follow relationships among events, and build
an understandable narrative from the available evidence. This
narrative should explain what has been observed, how it has
been interpreted, what evidence supports the interpretation,
and what remains unknown.

Such a system should not conceal uncertainty or attempt to
remove it by presenting a single, confident answer. On the
contrary, it should help clarify the boundary between what
we know and what we merely consider possible.

This objective can also be meaningful beyond the foreign
exchange market. Whenever we face a complex situation,
incomplete information, and decisions involving risk, structural
understanding may help us comprehend the situation more clearly.
The nature of the evidence, the criteria for evaluation, and
the consequences of error, however, differ from one field to
another.

In the foreign exchange market, we investigate this idea through
candlesticks, boxes, price structures, and narratives describing
market events over time. But the central problem is not simply
to build a collection of indicators or discover patterns that
predict the next price movement.

The real question is how to extract an understandable structure
from continuous and changing data, and how to use that structure
to make better-informed decisions without claiming certainty.

**We do not seek to eliminate uncertainty. We seek to understand
structure within it, without forgetting the limits of our
own understanding.**
